Opera

The Myth of the Simurgh, and why opera is the art form I love most.

The Myth of the Simurgh: The Quest of the Holy Grail

Let’s talk about Opera, the finest and most elegant art form. To appreciate Opera as the ultimate art form, we can draw from the Persian myth of the Simurgh, as the poet Attar told it in his twelfth-century Conference of the Birds. In this epic allegory, thousands of individual birds set out on a perilous pilgrimage to find the Simurgh—the legendary, godlike bird that represents ultimate wisdom and beauty. By the end of the grueling journey, only thirty exhausted birds remain. When they finally reach the end of their quest, they discover that there is no external deity. Instead, they look into a mirror and realize that they, the thirty birds, have fused to become the Simurgh—in Persian, si murgh means “thirty birds.” Their individual identities have dissolved, blending into a single, flawless, supreme entity. The seeker and the sought are one.

Opera is the cultural manifestation of the Simurgh.

It takes the ‘thirty birds’ of human expression: poetry, instrumental music, singing, acting, stagecraft, and dance, and forges them through a creative imagination. The individual arts shed their singularity and blend into a single, flawless, transcendent entity. Opera is not merely a collection of different arts; it is the collective evolution of those arts into their finest, most divine possible form, what the Western tradition calls a Gesamtkunstwerk. When you sit in the dark, and the first notes rise from the pit, you are not merely watching a play with music or a concert with costumes. You are witnessing the thirty birds become one.

To me, Italian Opera is that divine unity. It is where poetry, drama, orchestra, and the human soul fuse into a singular, supreme form. And at the absolute center of my Simurgh sits La Divina—Maria Callas.

Giuseppe Verdi and the Forge of Human Passion

If the journey toward the ultimate art form requires an initiation through fire, blood, and tears, then Giuseppe Verdi is the master of that earthly crucible. Verdi does not protect his characters, nor does he allow his audience the comfort of emotional detachment. His music is deeply grounded in human grit, political tension, and visceral reality. In the grand design of the opera house, Verdi represents the heavy, thrumming heartbeat of humanity, weaving individual voices into devastating tapestries of fate.

The Irony of the Mask: Rigoletto

In Rigoletto, Verdi explores the tragic collision between the grotesque and the sublime. The Opera operates as a valley of shattering dualities. Rigoletto, the deformed court jester, wears a jagged, mocking mask of cruelty to survive a corrupt society, while keeping his daughter, Gilda, locked away like an isolated bird of pure light.

When Gilda sings her coloratura masterpiece, “Caro nome,” the rapid, intricate scales are not empty vocal acrobatics; they are the sound of an innocent heart fluttering its wings in the first flush of love. But Verdi’s genius is unyielding. In the legendary Act 3 Quartet, he dissolves these isolated lives into a singular, overwhelming wave of tragedy. The voices of the predatory Duke, the seductive Maddalena, the vengeful Rigoletto, and the heartbroken Gilda fuse into a counterpoint in which human cruelty and pure innocence converge at the same point of destiny.

The Holy Grail of Self-Sacrifice: Callas in Lisbon (1958)

Nowhere does the Verdian crucible burn hotter than in the legendary 1958 Lisbon performance of La Traviata at the Teatro Nacional de São Carlos. This live recording stands as the holy grail of operatic history because Maria Callas—La Divina—completely strips away the operatic ego to channel a true martyrdom.

In Act 1, Violetta is a bird of prey, tossing off stratospheric coloratura fireworks that mask her hollow lifestyle. But by Act 2, as Germont demands she abandon her lover Alfredo for the sake of his family’s honor, Violetta begins to sacrifice her happiness, and her music sinks into desolation. During “Dite alla giovine,” Callas drops her voice into a suspension in the quiet theater like a fragile glass thread. Then comes the emotional epicenter of the Opera: “Amami, Alfredo!” It is not a pretty vocal showcase; it is a desperate, volcanic scream of human grief that shatters the boundaries of the theater.

By Act 3, the spiritual annihilation is complete. Callas thins her tone to a hollow whisper; she sounds like a woman whose soul is already standing at death’s door. Her reading of Germont’s letter is spoken with a chilling, dead-eyed resignation that outclasses any spoken-theater actress in history. After her devastating farewell, “Addio del passato,” she is joined by a young, fiercely elegant Alfredo Kraus, and their duet “Parigi, o cara” becomes a heartbreaking, fragile illusion of hope before her final collapse. Callas proves that the Simurgh is found not in mechanical vocal perfection, but in total, raw artistic self-sacrifice.

Vincenzo Bellini and the Ethereal Sanctuary

Vincenzo Bellini leaves the heavy, blood-soaked earth behind and carries us directly into the clouds. He is the absolute monarch of the Bel Canto (“beautiful singing”) era, a style that treats the human voice not as a dramatic weapon, but as an airborne, spiritual vessel.

In Bellini’s masterpiece, Norma, the terrifying coloratura demands are no longer athletic displays—they are a form of high priesthood. The legendary aria “Casta Diva” represents the ultimate manifestation of the infinite melodic line. As the druid priestess prays to the chaste moon goddess for peace, the music becomes an enchanting, seamless river of sound.

Bellini’s orchestrations are intentionally transparent, providing a delicate, glimmering canvas that places the entire burden of the divine on a single voice. Norma does not fight her orchestra; she rises completely above it, suspending time itself. For a lover of coloratura, this is the sky valley. This art form reaches the divine not by crashing down in tragedy, but by weightlessly dissolving into pure, celestial beauty.

The French Transmutation: Exoticism, Desire, and the Siren’s Sea

When we journey across the Alps into 19th-century French Opera, the spiritual quest takes on intoxicating, highly perfumed new colors. A lavish preoccupation replaces the raw, volcanic passion of Italy with exotic locales, complex, delicate psychological textures, and absolute vocal magic.

The Valley of Grounded Desire: Bizet’s Carmen

In Georges Bizet’s Carmen, the high coloratura stratosphere drops down into the rich, dangerous, rhythmic undercurrents of the earth. Carmen herself is a force of nature, an untamable bird that refuses to be caged. Through the hypnotic, swaying pull of the Habanera and the sharp, seductive danger of the Seguidilla, Bizet proves that Opera can achieve its transcendent unity not just through celestial prayers, but through the irresistible, fatal gravity of human desire. It is a completely different kind of magic—visceral, dark, and utterly intoxicating.

The Labyrinth of Silver Bells: Delibes’s Lakmé

While Léo Delibes’s Lakmé is explicitly rooted in the 19th-century Western fascination with an idealized, mysterious Orient, the piece transcends historical boundaries through the brilliant artistry of a modern vocal icon.

The centerpiece of the Opera, the famous “Bell Song” (“Où va la jeune Hindoue”), is a dizzying, perilous labyrinth of music. It demands rapid-fire staccatos, ethereal echo effects, and a stratospheric high E-natural that can easily sound like a mechanical, bloodless music box in lesser hands.

Natalie Dessay: Jumping the Boundless Blue

But Natalie Dessay is a siren of an entirely different order. When Dessay sings Lakmé, she strips away the porcelain veneer of the character and throws herself headfirst into a vast, turbulent ocean of raw human psychology.

Dessay’s coloratura is absolutely fearless. It is physically electric, emotionally exposed, and breathtakingly agile. She doesn’t just navigate the high notes; she uses them to map the boundaries of a soul caught between sacred duty and forbidden love. Listening to her is like willingly jumping off a cliff into a deep, endless blue sea of sound. The sheer waves of her voice submerge your ego, fulfilling the ultimate promise of the Simurgh myth: you lose yourself completely in the music, only to find your spirit fully integrated on the other side.

My Manifesto: The Seeker and the Sound

This is the sanctuary of my personal website, a space dedicated to tracking the divine frequency where the many separate threads of human creation weave into the supreme One.

I am a seeker captivated by the world of Italian Opera, the gymnastics of coloratura, and the transcendent art of the sublime. My personal map of this vast territory begins in the aching, vulnerable heart of Giuseppe Verdi, forever anchored by Maria Callas’s 1958 Lisbon La Traviata and the dark, biting ironies of Rigoletto. It takes flight into the weightless, celestial ether of Vincenzo Bellini’s Norma, where the soul suspends time in an infinite melodic line. And finally, it plunges into the intoxicating, perfumed waters of the French masters—from the dangerous, rhythmic fires of Bizet’s Carmen to the brilliant, echoing silver bells of Delibes’s Lakmé.

Guided by the electric, fearless artistry of my vocal sirens, the great sopranos, I gladly leap into that boundless sea of sound. It is a beautiful, terrifying depth from which I never wish to resurface. Here, amidst the high notes and the grand passions, the thirty birds find their wings, the mirror is cleared, and the divine form of art stands revealed.
